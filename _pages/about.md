I am a 5th-year PhD Candidate in Economics at the University of Innsbruck, currently visiting the University of Chicago. Using field experiments, I investigate how economic incentives and behavioral biases influence decisions in markets that impact sustainability and social welfare.

My dissertation, ***Repair and Reuse Economics***, focuses on two areas: reducing fraudulent behavior among repair service providers and understanding consumer reluctance toward purchasing second-hand goods. I design and implement field interventions to address market failures and behavioral barriers in both sectors.

My broader research agenda includes work on prosocial behavior, exploring the determinants of donations and volunteering, and gender economics, examining how gender shapes economic opportunities and choices.

### JMP: Environmental costs in second-hand clothing purchase: A voucher choice experiment

**[Download my Job Market Paper](files/jmp.pdf)**

<details>
<summary>Abstract</summary>
<p>Consumers may wish to act in environmentally responsible ways, yet informational barriers often prevent them from accurately understanding the true environmental costs of their consumption choices. In this paper, we investigate how environmental costs—in terms of CO2 emissions, electric energy consumption, or water usage—affect intention to engage in second-hand clothing consumption. We measure both stated changes in purchasing intention and incentivized choices via multiple price lists, eliciting willingness to accept (WTA) and willingness to pay (WTP) for vouchers at first-hand versus second-hand clothing stores. We draw on a large, nationally representative sample of Italians (N = 10,496). First, we find that information provision is effective in promoting second-hand adoption across all types of environmental costs, water-based information generating the strongest effect.  Second, we find that pro-environmental behavior amplifies responsiveness to the information, while need for uniqueness operates as a significant barrier, these individuals requiring approximately €2 additional compensation to switch to second-hand options. Third, local environmental conditions—including water scarcity, energy poverty, and air quality—do not systematically moderate treatment effects. We also document that store proximity influences baseline preferences but does not moderate the information provision effects. The results suggest that consumers react to multiple environmental indicators, showing especially strong sensitivity to water use compared with CO2, yet large segments of the public remain disengaged from sustainability information.</p>
</details>
